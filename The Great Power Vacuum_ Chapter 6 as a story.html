History, Chapter 6: Rise of Independent Regional Powers

The Great Power Vacuum

What happens when a giant empire cracks? Everybody rushes in.

Episode 1The giant falls

For a long time, most of India was held up by one enormous pillar: the Mughal empire. Then in 1707 the emperor Aurangzeb died, and the empire began to break apart. The pillar cracked.

When something that big collapses, it leaves an empty space behind it. As the Mughal empire began to disintegrate (break into pieces), the political vacuum (empty space of power) was filled by a number of independent and semi-independent regional powers (fully free and partly free kingdoms) such as Bengal, Awadh (Oudh), Hyderabad, Mysore and the Maratha, Sikh and Rajput states. F1

These new powers were made in two different ways:

Recipe 1, rebellion: some, like the Maratha kingdom, were products of rebellion against the Mughal authority.
Recipe 2, the governor walks away: others, like Bengal, were set up when governors of Mughal provinces took advantage of the decaying central authority (the weakening power in Delhi) and asserted their independence (declared themselves free).

Lab note: physics

Open an empty bottle underwater and water rushes in. A vacuum doesn't stay empty, because the pressure outside pushes something in. Power works the same way. When the Mughal "pressure" disappeared, local rulers rushed in to fill the gap.

Episode 2The Marathas: the strongest contender

The most important of all the regional powers was the Maratha kingdom. In fact, the Marathas alone had the power and the potential to establish an all-India empire upon the ashes of the Mughal empire. So they get the biggest part of our story.

The prisoner prince and the clever minister

Shahu, the grandson of Shivaji, had been imprisoned by Aurangzeb. He was only released after the emperor died in 1707. Straight away, a war of succession (a fight over who would be the next king) broke out among the Marathas over who should be king.

Shahu won the throne with the help of Balaji Vishwanath. As a reward for his services and loyalty, Balaji Vishwanath was given the post of Peshwa or chief minister (mukhya pradhan) in 1713.

Now the twist. Shahu was ease-loving and weak. He liked comfort more than ruling. Slowly he was reduced to the position of ceremonial head (a king only for show) of the Maratha kingdom, while the real power and authority was exercised by the Peshwa.

Think of a school where the principal only cuts ribbons at functions while the vice-principal actually runs everything. That was Shahu and his Peshwa.

This was the beginning of the second phase of Maratha history: a period of dominance of the Peshwas, who ruled the kingdom and transformed it into an empire (1713–1818). H1(a)

Three Peshwas, three superpowers

1. Balaji Vishwanath
The deal-maker

He took full advantage of the weaknesses of the Mughal administration to extract important concessions (get special rights) and increase the power and prestige of the Marathas:

All the territories that once belonged to Shivaji, but had been conquered by Aurangzeb, were restored to Shahu. The Marathas also got the right to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi (two kinds of tax) from six Mughal provinces in the Deccan.

In 1719, Balaji Vishwanath marched to Delhi to help the Sayyid brothers overthrow the Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar. Imagine that: the Marathas were now strong enough to help decide who sat on the Mughal throne!

He made the Marathas a formidable power (a very strong power) and laid the foundations of the hereditary, dynastic rule of the Peshwas. That means the job of Peshwa would now pass from father to son. He was succeeded by his son, Baji Rao I.

Lab note: maths

Chauth was one-fourth of a province's revenue and sardeshmukhi was an extra one-tenth. If a province collected 1,000 gold coins, how many went to the Marathas?

Answer: 250 + 100 = 350 coins, which is 35%. Now multiply that by six provinces.

2. Baji Rao I
The military genius

Baji Rao I was a military genius. Under his bold and dynamic leadership, the Maratha kingdom was transformed into the Maratha empire after a series of brilliant campaigns against the Mughals and other rivals. H1(b)(i)

During his time, four prominent Maratha families rose to power: the Sindhia, the Bhonsle, the Holkar and the Gaekwad.
memory trick: "Some Brave Horsemen Gallop"

How the Maratha confederacy was built

The empire was now too big for one man to control directly. So the Peshwas divided the erstwhile Mughal provinces (former Mughal provinces) into "spheres of influence" (areas of control) and placed them under the chiefs of these Maratha families.

These chiefs enjoyed maximum autonomy (freedom to run things their own way) within their regions. The Peshwa was the official head of this loose union of Maratha chiefs, which is called the Maratha confederacy. H1(c)

Lab note: chemistry

In a molecule, atoms joined by strong bonds are hard to separate. Atoms held by weak forces can be pulled apart easily. The confederacy was a loose union, closer to the weak kind. Each chief was strong in his own region, but the whole thing was only loosely held together at the centre.

3. Balaji Baji Rao
The expander

Baji Rao I was succeeded by his 18-year-old son, Balaji Baji Rao. When Shahu died, the Peshwa became the official head of the Maratha empire. No more pretending! Poona (now Pune), the headquarters of the Peshwas, became the capital of the Maratha empire.

Balaji Baji Rao continued the policy of expansion. During his reign, the Maratha empire reached the heights of its power and glory. In the north, the Marathas became the power behind the Mughal throne. H1(b)(ii)

The crash at Panipat, 1761

The Marathas kept pushing north and conquered Punjab. This brought them into direct conflict with the Afghan ruler, Ahmad Shah Abdali. A major struggle for mastery over north India (fight to control north India) began.

The Maratha army was decisively defeated by Ahmad Shah Abdali at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761. The Maratha ambition of replacing the Mughal empire lay scattered on the plains of Panipat.

But here's the strange part. The Afghans, too, failed to capitalize on their victory (did not make use of their win). They won the battle and then went home without taking over. Both big players were tired out. The conditions were ideal for the entry of another powerful contender for supremacy (a rival for the top position): the English East India Company. G1

Answer frame for G1: why is the Third Battle of Panipat significant?
  1. The Maratha army was decisively defeated by Ahmad Shah Abdali.
  2. The Maratha ambition of replacing the Mughal empire was destroyed.
  3. The Afghans also failed to capitalize on their victory.
  4. This created ideal conditions for the English East India Company to rise.

Episode 3Bengal: rich, but with a weak immune system

Bengal was one of the richest provinces of the Mughal empire. Remember that word, "richest". The textbook's true/false question tries to trick you with "poorest".

Murshid Quli Khan: the governor who walked away

As Mughal power declined, the governor of Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan, took advantage of the situation to free himself from imperial control (the emperor's control). He was clever about it. He continued to pay annual tribute (a yearly payment to show loyalty) to the Mughal empire, so Delhi stayed happy, but he began to rule Bengal independently. F2

Murshid Quli Khan was an excellent administrator and a man of exceptional abilities. He freed Bengal from external and internal dangers and established stability, peace and prosperity in the state. H2(a)

Alivardi Khan: the one who saw the danger

The most capable among his successors was Alivardi Khan. He recognized the importance of promoting trade and encouraged merchants, Indian as well as foreign, to trade in Bengal.

But he was careful. He did not allow the French and British merchants to fortify their factories (build walls and defences around their trading posts) in Chandernagore (now Chandannagar) and Calcutta (now Kolkata). Trade, yes. Forts, no. H2(b)

"Factory" here means a trading post and warehouse, not a place with machines.

Where it went wrong

The nawabs of Bengal failed to understand the true nature and ulterior motives of the British trading company (its hidden plans). They believed that a trading company could never challenge their authority. No efforts were made to reorganize and strengthen the army. Corruption was rampant (everywhere), and even high ranking officials were susceptible to bribes (could easily be bought with money). H2(c)

These weaknesses made Bengal a happy hunting ground for the aggressive and imperialistic ambitions of the British (an easy target for the British, who wanted to rule other lands).

Lab note: biology

Your immune system works when it can recognize an invader, fight it with strong defences, and trust its own cells. Bengal's nawabs failed at all three. They didn't recognize the British as a threat, their "white blood cells" (the army) were weak, and their own officials could be bribed. Alivardi Khan was the one part of the immune system that did react, which is why he stopped the forts being built.

Episode 4Awadh: peace grows culture

The foundation of the independent state of Awadh was laid by Saadat Khan, the governor of the Mughal province of Awadh. He was intelligent, efficient and daring. He established an efficient administration, maintained law and order, introduced revenue reforms (better ways of collecting taxes) and strengthened his army.

The family line goes like this:

Saadat Khan → his nephew Safdar Jung → his son Shuja-ud-Daulah

Safdar Jung brought stability, peace and prosperity to Awadh. Then came Shuja-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Awadh.

Here's the key idea. The prolonged period of peace and economic prosperity (a long time of peace and wealth) during the reign of the nawabs stimulated the growth of a refined and distinct Lucknavi culture (encouraged a graceful, special Lucknow style). Lucknow, the capital city, blossomed into a centre for creative and performing arts, literature and architecture. F3

Lab note: biology pun

In the lab, a bacterial culture only grows well in a stable medium: steady temperature, enough nutrients, no disturbance. Human culture is similar. Awadh had peace (stability) and prosperity (nutrients), so art, music, poetry and buildings could grow. That's your F3 answer in one line: stability → growth of culture.

Episode 5Mysore: the tigers of the south

Go back in time a bit. The collapse of the powerful Vijayanagar empire in the 17th century gave rise to a number of independent kingdoms who were constantly at war with one another. The most important of these was the kingdom of Mysore, which somehow managed to preserve its independence despite repeated attacks by neighbouring kingdoms. H3(a)

In the middle of the 18th century, Mysore, under the leadership of Hyder Ali, emerged as one of the most powerful kingdoms in the south.

Hyder Ali: from soldier to ruler

Hyder Ali was a man of exceptional qualities and abilities. He was not born a prince. He rose from the ranks of an ordinary soldier in the Mysore army to the position of commander by sheer dint of merit and determination (only through his own talent and hard work).

In 1761, he overthrew the government and established his authority over Mysore. Under his rule, Mysore became a prosperous and powerful state and a formidable adversary of the English East India Company in the south (a very strong enemy). H3(b)

Date trick: 1761 twice! The Marathas crashed at Panipat in the north, and Hyder Ali rose in the south, in the same year.

Tipu Sultan: the Tiger of Mysore

Hyder Ali was succeeded by his son, Tipu Sultan, who proved to be a worthy successor. He introduced many reforms to overhaul and reorganize the administrative machinery (completely change and rearrange the way the government worked). He modernized the army and strengthened the economy by encouraging agriculture and modern trade and industry. He posed the most serious challenge to the rise of British power in India. H3(c)

Compare him with Bengal: Bengal's nawabs made no efforts to strengthen their army. Tipu modernized his army and strengthened his economy, which is why he was such a big challenge to the British.

Tipu Sultan's swords are famous for their craftsmanship (skill in making). They were made from high-quality Wootz steel, decorated with tiger motifs (tiger designs) that stood for his title, the "Tiger of Mysore". They carried inscriptions in Persian and were often inlaid with gold.

Lab note: chemistry and physics

Chemistry: steel is iron with a small amount of carbon. Wootz steel from South India was made by melting iron with carbon-rich material in sealed clay pots, which put a lot of carbon into it and gave it a beautiful wavy pattern. Scientists studying old swords made from this kind of steel have found tiny carbon structures inside them.

Physics: Hyder Ali and Tipu also used war rockets with iron tubes, which could fly much farther than older rockets with paper or bamboo tubes. The British were so impressed that they studied captured Mysore rockets to design their own. Think about why a strong iron tube would let you pack in more gunpowder.
